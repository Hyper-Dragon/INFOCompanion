*What came in the Zork I box, transcribed from the package itself.*

The box held: the game disk (its label reads **ZORK I / IZ1-AP1 — Apple II, 16-sector disk**); the
**instruction manual** (the pages here run from 4 to 11: Loading ZORK, Talking to ZORK, Rules and
Strategies, and the reference section); the history booklet, *The History of the Great
Underground Empire*, a schoolbook-thin thing of four chapters; the booklet's own **library
card**; and the **map**.

## The box, in its own words

**The front** is the word **ZORK** cut in stone blocks, with an open door standing where the O
should be — light on the other side of it, and a way down below — over a line promising that your
greatest challenge lies ahead, and downwards. Below that: *"An INTERLOGIC™ prose adventure"*.

**The back** is the pitch, and it is worth knowing because it tells you what kind of game this is
meant to be:

> You're on the threshold of a whole new experience. For ahead of you is the extraordinary
> trilogy of the Great Underground Empire... **ZORK I: The Great Underground Empire** confronts
> you with perils and predicaments ranging from the mystical to the macabre, as you strive to
> discover the **Twenty Treasures of ZORK** and escape with them and your life.

It promises that the three games can be played in any order, and it brags about the parser in a
way that dates it: the game recognises over 600 words, "including adjectives"; if you type
something it does not understand, it tells you *which word* is the trouble; and it can take a
command like **"FLOYD, PUT THE MONSTER AND THE GREEN MONKEY INTO THE IRON CAGE THEN LOCK THE DOOR
AND THROW AWAY THE KEY"** — "a piece of cake for our games". The last word is the series motto:
*"All words. No pictures."* A 90-day limited warranty is printed along the bottom.

## The manual — what a player had in their hands

**Loading and talking.** Load it and you get the copyright notice, a serial number, and the
game's opening view — the manual prints the screen you will be looking at when play begins: an
open field west of a white house, and a small mailbox, and its own example command is `OPEN THE
MAILBOX`. From then on the game sits behind a `>` prompt: type what you want to do, press
RETURN, read what happens. ZORK behaves as though every sentence begins with *"I want to…"*.
**All words are distinguished by their first six letters** — so `ENCYCLOPEDIA` is understood as
`ENCYCL` — and ZORK uses many more words than it "understands", so now and then it prints a
reaction you could not have asked for and could not ask for again.

**Sentences.** A sentence needs a verb (or a command like `AGAIN`), usually with an object. One
direct object, one indirect object, and multiple objects joined by **AND** or a comma. Several
sentences at once must be separated by a period or by **THEN** — `TAKE BOOK. N. DROP BOOK AND
CANDLE`, or `TAKE THE BOOK THEN N. DROP BOOK AND CANDLE`. Only two questions exist: **WHAT** and
**WHERE** (`WHERE IS THE GOLD?`, `WHAT IS A GRUE?`). If you are vague, ZORK asks what you meant
(`WITH WHAT?`) and you can answer in one word.

**The commands**, as the manual lists them — usable whenever the prompt appears:

`AGAIN` (do the last thing again), `BRIEF` (describe rooms only the first time), `DIAGNOSE`
(a medical report on yourself), `INVENTORY` (what you are carrying), `LOOK` (look around; `L` will
do), `QUIT`, `RESTART`, `RESTORE`, `SAVE`, `SCORE`, `SCRIPT` / `UNSCRIPT` (start and stop printing
a transcript), `SUPERBRIEF` (room names only), `VERBOSE` (full descriptions every time), `VERSION`,
`WAIT` (let time pass and see what happens).

**Common verbs**, as the manual lists them: attack, burn, climb, close, dig, drink, drop, eat,
enter, examine, follow, get in, kill, list, look, move, open, pour, pull, push, read, save, take,
throw, turn, walk, where is. Directions are `NORTH`/`N`, `SOUTH`/`S`, `EAST`/`E`, `WEST`/`W`,
`NE`, `NW`, `SE`, `SW`, `UP`/`U`, `DOWN`/`D`, and — in the right places — `ENTER`/`EXIT` or
`IN`/`OUT`. **There are 10 possible directions**, and the manual says plainly what that is for:
passages in the underground "twist and turn unpredictably", you cannot assume that going north and
back south returns you anywhere, and *"it is essential that you draw a map. Some of the problems…
cannot be solved without mapping."*

**What the replies mean**, because the manual prints the list:

- `BEG PARDON?` — nothing was typed before RETURN.
- `I DON'T KNOW THE WORD` — it is not in this game's vocabulary (a synonym may work).
- `IT'S TOO DARK TO SEE.` — no light.
- `I CAN'T SEE ANY ___ HERE.` — the object you named is not here.
- `YOU MUST SUPPLY A VERB` — unless you are answering a question, every sentence needs one.
- `MULTIPLE OBJECTS CANNOT BE USED WITH <verb>` — some verbs take one object only.
- `TOO MANY NOUN CLAUSES` — a sentence with more than one direct or indirect object.

**Rules and strategies**, in the manual's own voice:

- **Score and rank.** ZORK keeps score; points come for finding treasure, for **depositing it
  safely in the trophy case**, for certain actions and for visiting certain places. There is a
  penalty for getting yourself killed. A full score is **350 points**, and quitting tells you your
  score and your rank.
- **Light and weapons**, because "caves are often dark" and "dungeons may have unfriendly beings".
- **Containers**: some hold other things, some open and close, some are transparent, most have a
  limit, and some objects have surfaces you can put things on.
- **Reading material is scattered around** — books, labels, engravings — and some of it is useful.
- **Beware the thief**: "a dastardly anti-social type and a skilled pickpocket". Watch your
  valuables when he is near.
- **Fighting is real**: characters fight back, some are stronger than others, and coming off
  second-best leaves you "somewhat the worse for wear" — `DIAGNOSE` tells you where you stand.
- **Vehicles** exist that carry you where your feet cannot, "and needless to say, you face great
  personal peril".
- **Time passes only in response to your input** — nothing happens while you sit and think, so
  you can plan slowly. (You can picture a giant clock that ticks once per move.)
- **Saving** is a snapshot: `SAVE`, then a filename, and you can `RESTORE` any time, even
  mid-play. The manual advises prudence: save before a tricky or dangerous part.
- **Success has no order**: "There is no 'correct' order for solving problems", some have more
  than one solution, and some need not be solved at all. It even recommends playing with another
  person, which is exactly what we are doing.

## The history booklet

The cover calls it *The History of the Great Underground Empire*: a booklet by **Froboz
Mumbar**, from the **Frobozz Magic Book Company**, whose title page reads *"Copied right in the
year 896. All rights reversed."* It is written as a children's history book, chapter by chapter,
complete with a schoolbook's questions at the end of each one and a list of further reading that
is entirely made up. Four chapters:

1. **The Bellicose King.** In 659 GUE the Kingdom of Quendor — seven-and-a-half provinces of rope
   and mosquito netting, the thirty-first year of Zilbo III, at the end of a dynasty six centuries
   old — passes to **Duncanthrax** on the final day of the year. What became of Zilbo is disputed;
   the evidence suggests exile to a villa, where he invented the card game *Double Fanucci*.
   Duncanthrax's own past is murky (one petition looks suspiciously signed by him; one legend
   calls him a demon, another a rope salesman), but his temperament is not: cruelty and
   bloodthirstiness earn him the nickname **the Bellicose**, and within three years his armies
   hold everything between the Great Sea and the Kovalli Desert. Of the exile the chapter prints
   a picture of an ancient villa on the outskirts of **Mithicus**, much like the one the old king
   is said to have finished his days in.
2. **An Empire Goes Underground.** In 665, at the battle of **Fort Griffspotter**, Duncanthrax
   destroys the armada of **Antharia** — the world's sea power, famous for its **granola mines** —
   and gains its shipyards and its recipe. The trouble: no one in Quendor likes granola. Then a
   navy returning from across the Great Sea reports caverns and tunnels on the far shore, full of
   gnomes, trolls and other magical races — **all of whom love granola**. What Duncanthrax takes
   from the story is a builder's thought: if caverns are possible in nature, they can be *dug*. In
   668 he founds the **Frobozz Magic Construction Company** (forerunner of the modern FrobozzCo
   International) and sets it to expanding the natural caverns and digging new ones beneath his own
   castle, **Egreth**. By his death in 688 he rules virtually everything in the known world, above
   ground and below. The chapter ends with three pie charts of royal spending at 580, 680 and 780
   GUE — caverns, roads and bridges, and the Royal Bureaucracy — under Bozbo IV, Duncanthrax, and
   Dimwit Flathead.
3. **The Flatheads.** Duncanthrax's descendants take the surname **Flathead** for "obscure
   reasons", and the Empire goes quiet for a century until 770 and **Dimwit Flathead**, its worst
   ruler and its most colourful. His coronation took thirteen years to plan, a year and a half to
   hold, and cost twelve times the Empire's GNP. He renames Quendor **The Great Underground
   Empire**, the Great Sea the **Flathead Ocean**, and moves the capital from Egreth in the
   westlands to **Flatheadia** in the eastlands. Taxes rise to just over 98%, and the money goes
   on his whims: **Flood Control Dam Number Three** — "a massive edifice with virtually no useful
   purpose, since it never rains underground" — the **Royal Museum** for the crown jewels, the
   defoliation of four hundred thousand acres of forest in the **Fublio Valley** to raise a
   nine-bloit-high statue of himself ("A truly great ruler is larger than life"), and the
   enormous **granola smelters of Plumbat**. Before his death in 789 he is rumoured to be planning
   his greatest dream: a new continent in the centre of the Flathead Ocean, its outline a
   gigantic reproduction of his own face.
4. **Fall of the Empire.** The Flatheads who follow keep up the tradition of excess, and the
   money goes on parties and holidays instead of works. In **883** the Empire collapses, the Royal
   Treasury is sacked, and everybody moves somewhere else. The chapter carries the **Kings of
   Quendor** — the Entharion dynasty (Entharion the Wise at 0, through Zylon the Aged, Zilbo I and
   II, the Fzorts, four Bozbos, and Zilbo III, who loses it all) and the Flathead dynasty
   (Duncanthrax the Bellicose, Belwit the Flat, Frobwit the Flatter, Timberthrax, Phloid,
   Mumberthrax, Dimwit, Loowit, Dunewit, Barbawit, Wurb, and Idwit Oogle Flathead, who ends the
   line in 883) — and a timeline of the Empire's most important political and cultural events.

Each chapter ends with **Questions, Discussions, Projects and Further Readings**, straight-faced
throughout and sharper at the edges than a schoolbook should be: stage a mock peace conference,
one classmate playing Duncanthrax while the rest argue him out of invading; find Quendor on a
modern map and work out which of its natural features helped it conquer everything around it;
collect ten **zorkmids** from everyone on your block to erect a giant statue of yourself — *"use
force if necessary"*, and watch whether your neighbours start to resent you; and read up on it
all in books that do not exist — *The New Year's Revolt* (Jezbar Foolion),
*Construction of the Empire* (Mumboz Agrippa), *Wouldn't It Be Fun To Live Underground?* (Lorissa
Prob), *The Seventy Year Snidgel* (Harv Dornfrob), *The Lives of the Twelve Flatheads* (Boswell
Barwell), *The Dark Age of Frobozz* (Sybar Zeebin) and *So You Want To Sack an Empire* (Uncle
Frobizzmus). The booklet closes with the game's own invitation: the long-abandoned Empire is
yours for the exploring, a thief and a troll and other troglodytes are still at home in it, and
before you can emerge victorious you must collect its treasures and bring them back to daylight —
for which you will need courage, cunning and logic.

## The library card

The booklet's own borrowing card, from the **Festeron Town Library**: a ruled card with the
printed line *"Please take care of me... library books belong to everyone."* and a column of
date stamps dated in the Empire's own years — 02/06/897, 03/03/898, 09/19/900, 06/07/903,
04/04/917, 02/21/948 — including two that cannot be dates at all (the 34th of January, the 32nd
of January) and one that runs four thousand years past all the others. It is the card that was
in the back of the book that came in the box.

## The map

A fold-out map on parchment-coloured stock, drawn by hand as the Empire's own chart of itself,
with the sea, the mountains and the Empire's hand-lettering given equal care.

- The **sea** fills one whole side — drawn with wavy water-lines — and it is inhabited: a **sea
  serpent** curls and splashes in one corner, and a **great fish**, all fin and curl, swims
  further down the coast.
- A **compass** stands over the water on a cross of four arrows, each arm ending in an arrowhead
  with a letter at its tip.
- **Snow-capped mountains** run across the far side with foothills drawn peak by peak.
- **Deep forest** covers the middle of the map and a great crescent of the bottom: drawn tree by
  tree, with clearings and tracks through it, and one hillside worked into terraces or
  walkways.
- About midway, a **cave mouth** in the hills, ringed with the artist's burst of lines — and the
  **river** issuing straight out of it: stony at first, then a wide winding river running the
  length of the map to the sea.
- Near the coast, **a single tree** stands on its own, its canopy drawn as a great scalloped
  cloud.
- **Reeds and wet channels** mark one far corner of the land.
- A **handful of labels** — boxed, each one set against the land or water it names, one of them
  directly above the cave mouth — and a curling **title scroll** across the bottom.
- All of that lettering is in the Empire's own hand, and it is a strange one: several of the
  letters are ours drawn back-to-front — an *S* like **Ƨ**, an *E* like **Ɛ**, an *R* like **Я**,
  an *F* like **ꟻ** — in every corner of the map and on the scroll alike, so the labels read as a
  foreign script until the eye turns the letters round. The booklet is where the Empire's places
  and people are spelled in our own letters.
- The map is the Empire's view of the world above ground. It is good for the shape of the land,
  and it does **not** map the underground you will spend the evening in — that is a map the player
  draws, which is what the manual tells you to do on its first page of strategy.

## How these papers are meant to be used

Everything here is what a person had in their hands before they typed anything: the manual that
defines the vocabulary and the grammar of every sentence you will type, the history that gives
the world its names and its jokes, the map of the surface, and the card in the back of the book.
None of it is a walkthrough. The game's own advice is the shape of it — light, weapons, a case for
the treasure, a save before anything dangerous, and a sheet of paper for your own map — and the
rest is yours to find out.
