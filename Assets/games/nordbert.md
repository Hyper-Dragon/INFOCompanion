*What came in the Nord and Bert box, transcribed from the package itself.*

The box held: the game disk; the instruction manual (not among these scans); and the reason this
page is mostly cartoons — **a booklet of original Kevin Pope cartoons**, which the back of the box
advertises as coming in every package. The booklet is not decoration. Every cartoon in it takes an
ordinary idiom and draws it literally, which is the whole engine of the game, and reading them
before you play tells you what kind of puzzle you are about to be handed.

## The box, in its own words

**The front** is set on a school exercise book: pale green horizontal rules, with the title in
heavy black letters across three lines — **Nord and Bert Couldn't Make Head or Tail of It** — and,
in green italic beneath, the subtitle that says exactly what the game is:

> *Eight Tales of Clichés, Spoonerisms, and Other Verbal Trickery*

Below the title, a Kevin Pope drawing: a red barn on a fieldstone footing, a rail fence, and two
farmers in overalls and straw hats — one carrying a milking pail, both standing and looking at a
black-and-white cow, which is grazing, unbothered, on the grass. Nobody in it is doing anything
unusual, which is the joke: they could be a cliché and you would have to be told which one.

**The back** is a single wide cartoon, captioned along the top in the same lettering as the cover:

> **Nick never got any chicks, until he bought those Infocom Games.**

It shows a man in a checked shirt, beaming, holding up a boxed game and saying *"Hey girls… my
place, ten minutes…"* to two women with their hands clasped, one of them saying *"Ohhhh, Nick."*
Under it, in small print, the point:

> Invite your friends over to play *Nord and Bert* and check out the booklet of original Kevin Pope
> cartoons included in each package.

## The Kevin Pope cartoon booklet

Each page is one idiom drawn at face value, titled in hand-lettering. The nine in the scans:

* **"Nick never got any chicks, until he bought those Infocom Games."** — the back cover, above.
* **"HOME ON THE RANGE"** — a two-storey clapboard house with a lawn, a hedge, a dog and two people
  with a hose, built on one ring of an electric cooker's hotplate, in the middle of the other three
  burners. One burner is lit.
* **"All ALONE ON A DESSERTED ISLE"** — a castaway in a green barrel on a small island, a palm tree
  overhead, and the sand covered in cakes, pies, puddings, a jelly, a tin, an egg and a slice of
  watermelon: an island made of dessert, and the man is holding a spoon.
* **"Old Farmer Brown is always worried sick till the cows come home"** — the farmer and his dog
  watching as three cows ride home in a yellow taxi, horns and all.
* **"THE MAYOR MEETS THE PUBLIC DEFENDER"** — two large men in suits swinging at each other
  through a wall, having both walked up to the same spot from opposite sides: *"Nice to meet you"*
  — *"Oh, yeah."*
* **"A HARDY TICK / A TARDY HICK"** — a two-panel joke of exactly the kind the game runs on: a tick
  on the ground under a five-thousand-pound pyramid weight (*a hardy tick*), and a yokel running
  late for something with a briefcase (*a tardy hick*).
* **"THE CHEF'S SURPRISE"** — a chef in a tall toque pulling a cherry-topped cake from the oven
  while, behind him on the table, a joint of meat has grown legs and is walking away holding a
  knife.
* **"CHET AND HIS NEW BUSH JACKET"** — a man in sunglasses and a hat out walking, wearing a jacket
  that is a bush, with a rifle under his arm.
* **"THE OLD PIE IN THE FACE ROUTINE IS QUICKLY COUNTERED BY THE OLD BOWLING BALL TO THE FACE
  ROUTINE"** — one man flinching away from a thrown pie, another going over backwards from a
  bowling ball that has come up on a cable.
* **"THE LIBRARY"** — a woman in a yellow dress looking along a shelf so tightly packed that the
  books are one continuous band of gold spines, saying *"Hmmm… let's see, a nice book or
  something…"*, and the shelf answering: *"Books? Books? There aren't any books here!…"*

## What this tells you about the game

The cartoons are the tutorial. Every one of them is a *saying* — a cliché, a phrase, a pun on
words that sound alike — taken seriously as a physical situation, and the game's eight tales are
built on that same move. So the way to read a puzzle here is not *"what does this room contain?"*
but **"what is this room a figure of speech for?"**, and a screen that looks nonsensical is very
often a sentence whose words have been rearranged or replaced by their near-twins. Note the
subtitle's three ingredients in particular: **clichés**, **spoonerisms** (swapping the initial
sounds of two words), and other verbal trickery — and note that a spoonerism changes letters, so
words on the screen may be misspellings that still mean something.

Two practical notes. The instruction manual for this game was **not** among the scans, so the
command list a player had is not reproduced here; nothing in these scans overrides the standard
Infocom command set. And the box and booklet name no author or release number, so this page cannot
either — use the `VERSION` reply for that.
