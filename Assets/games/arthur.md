*What came in the Arthur box, transcribed from the package itself.*

The box held: the game disk; the instruction manual (the pages scanned here are 2–7 — Merlyn's
greeting, the preface, the interface, mapping, hints, the commands and the sample transcript); and
the **Book of Hours** — a small illuminated manuscript, which the box describes as having been
*"magically cast into each and every Arthur package"*, and which is the game's own opening
props rather than a manual. Its frontispiece is **King Lot's bookplate**, which tells you what
kind of story this is before you read a word of the game.

As with the other boxes in this collection, note which edition was scanned: this is the **Amiga
"Infocom Graphic"** release, sold with the split-screen windows, mouse support and on-screen
hints (all described below). The disk here may be the plain-text release, in which case the
pictures will not appear; the story, the mapping and the hints are the game's own.

## The box, in its own words

**The front**: the title **Arthur** in blue Gothic letters, *THE QUEST FOR EXCALIBUR* beneath it,
*By Bob Bates* at the top corner, and cover art signed **Greg Hildebrandt**. The painting is the
throne room — a boy in white lifting a blazing sword before a hall of kneeling and standing
kings, a great disc of light behind him. In the corner, the same Amiga starburst as the Shōgun
box: *"WITH ON-SCREEN HINTS AND SPECTACULAR GRAPHICS."*

**The back** opens with the sword's own inscription in blue Gothic:

> **"Whoso pulleth out this sword of this stone, is rightwise king born of all England."**

and then sets the problem, in the only place it is set out plainly:

> In the days before Camelot, when magic and evil rule England, a sword sheathed in stone appears
> in a quiet churchyard. Engraved upon it are the words which form your destiny. For you are
> Arthur, the son and true heir of the High King, Uther Pendragon.
>
> But there is more to asserting your claim than wresting free the sword. **The sword in the stone
> disappears — stolen by the evil King Lot.** To win it back, you must develop the kingly
> qualities of wisdom, chivalry, and experience, qualities that also prove you worthy of the
> throne. And you must do it all **within three days**, or Lot will usurp your legacy.
>
> You are assisted by Merlyn the Enchanter, who grants you the power to **transform yourself into
> a variety of animals**. Now you can explore the kingdom as no one has before: from the sky, from
> rivers and lakes, from on — and under — the earth. How you use this power, and how you solve
> your myriad puzzles that confront you, will determine your success in your quest.

Four bullets follow, and each is a feature worth knowing about:

* *"Merlyn's crystal ball reveals helpful hints."*
* *"The **undo** command lets you rescind even the most foolhardy of decisions."*
* *"Optional on-screen mapping gives your quest direction."*
* *"A medieval **Book of Hours** has been magically cast into each and every Arthur package, along
  with the game disk and complete instructions."*

The publisher's block: Infocom, 125 CambridgePark Drive, Cambridge, MA 02140; available for IBM
and 100% compatibles, Macintosh, Apple II series, and Amiga; © 1989 Infocom, Inc.; distributed by
MEDIAGENIC; ISBN 0-87321-471-4. Its "Screen shots shown are from the Macintosh II."

## The setup, from the manual

The manual opens with a poem, **"Greetings"**, signed *"My name is Merlinus Ambrosius, but men
call me Merlin."* It is the game's backstory and it is told by the man who will spend the game
testing you:

> Aye, I live still, even unto this present day.
> And I shall live on into worlds which you do not dream of,
> but which I already see.
>
> Legends tell you
> that I was beguiled into a trap, imprisoned in a hollow hill,
> a doddering old man blinded by misguided love
> for a beautiful yet evil sorceress.
>
> **Imprisoned I am, but not blinded — not now, or ever.**
>
> But let me tell you of those days.
> In a time almost before memory, there was a battle among the gods.
> **The evil demon Nudd was defeated and chained to a golden throne below a mountain.**
> Manacled though he was, his evil still extended beyond the mountain, making the surrounding
> land barren, and polluting everything it touched.
> The enchantment he had cast over the Lady of the Lake had such strength that even my gods were
> powerless to break it.
>
> Then a new god came into the land.
> His power was so great that the spell over the lady could be broken by the mere touch of the
> hawthorn flower that was holy to him, the one that bloomed only on the anniversary of his birth.
>
> When Arthur was born,
> he learned to embrace the power of the new god.
> But he also knew that the old gods, my gods, were not dead.
> **Only thus was he able to gain the sword.**
>
> So look now at those days through the eyes of the boy.
> Learn of England's need for him and how he came to write his name in legend.
> I say to you, the day shall come when the world has need of him once more. On that day
> shall I break these icy bonds and rise to my destiny
> — to bring new life, through fire and glory, to the bravest and truest knight the world has
> ever known: to Arthur — the once and future king.

The **Preface to the Story** then states the terms of the game, and they match the box:

> It has been many years since King Uther has died, and his once-proud kingdom is in danger of
> coming apart at the seams. No one has been able to draw the legendary sword from the stone. No
> one has been able to unite the country against the invasion of the Saxons. The people grow
> weary of life without a High King, and they are ready to accept a usurper on the throne, even
> though they know he is not the rightful heir.
>
> In *Arthur*, you play the role of the young king who has grown up in ignorance of his heritage.
> The time has come for you to draw Excalibur from the stone and rise up to defend Britain. But
> before you can do so, you must **prove to Merlin that you are ready to take up the burden of
> kingship**. As you progress through the game, you must **earn the wisdom, experience, and
> chivalry points** that will demonstrate to Merlin that you are indeed ready to claim your
> birthright.

So: three days, three kinds of points, and a wizard who has to be satisfied before the story will
let you win. **Note the tension in the front matter itself** — Nudd is chained *below a mountain*
and his evil still spreads, and there is a **Bottomless Pit of Despair** the hint system knows
about (see below); if a pit of despair is mentioned anywhere in this game, that is why.

## The interface, and what the screen is

This release's manual gives a whole section to a new screen layout, and it matters to anyone
watching rather than typing:

> The lower part of the screen contains the command line and the traditional written account of
> what happens to you during the game.
>
> The upper part of the screen contains your choice of any one of the following six windows.
> (Use the function keys specified to bring up these windows.)

* **Graphics** (function key 1) — *the default*; *"No matter how far afield you roam, you will
  always be able to get a picture of where you are."*
* **Map** (2) — *"The darkened square marks the room that you are currently in. The empty squares
  are rooms that you have previously explored. The compass rose displays all legitimate exits
  from your current location."*
* **Inventory** (3) — what you are carrying.
* **Score** (4) — *"represented here, both in bar charts and in numbers."*
* **Room Description** (5) — the LOOK text without spending a turn.
* **Text Only** (6) — *"For the hard-core, give-me-text-or-give-me-death gamer who would rather
  die than look at a picture. Return once more to those thrilling days of yesteryear — when men
  were men, text was text, and graphics got eaten by grues."*

Function keys: Apple IIe/IIc/IIgs — Closed Apple/Option with keys 1–6; Macintosh — Command with
1–6; **Amiga — F1 through F6**; IBM-PC and compatibles — F1 through F6. With a mouse (Amiga,
Apple IIGS, Macintosh, or an IBM with a Microsoft-compatible mouse) you can click an adjacent
room or a point on the compass rose to move, and click through the hint menu.

## The hints, and the map, and how the manual says to use them

The hints are reached in the fiction: *"simply go to Merlin's cave and look inside his crystal
ball (or look into the crystal of the torque that Merlin drops on the ground at the beginning of
the game)."* Then the instructions on your screen. Two things about them are worth keeping:

> The hint system tries to be smart about where you have been and what you have seen. If you
> haven't seen or heard about the **Bottomless Pit of Despair**, then it will not show up as a
> hint topic until you do. This is to prevent you from learning about something's existence
> inadvertently when you consult the hint system on another matter. Thus, when you start the
> game, the list of topics in the hint system will be very small. But don't panic! As you
> progress, there will be more and more things you can consult the hint system about.
>
> …We strongly recommend that you look at only one hint at a time. Avoid the temptation to use
> the hints too often; this inevitably spoils, or at least lessens, the fun of solving a puzzle.
>
> If you don't have the willpower to stop looking at the hints, you can type **HINTS OFF**. This
> will deactivate the on-screen hints (unless you RESTART or RESTORE to an earlier point).

**The map is on-screen**, which is a real difference from the hand-drawn sheets in the other
boxes: *"The map will show only those locations which you've already visited, as well as the
then-current legitimate exits from those locations… You may still want to draw your own map to
keep track of where objects are found, and to be able to look at the entire Arthur geography at
once."*

## The commands, as the manual lists them

Standard Infocom set, with the new interface's additions starred:

* **AGAIN (G)**, **BRIEF**, **COLOR**, **INVENTORY (I)**, **LOOK (L)**, **OOPS (O)**,
  **QUIT (Q)**, **RESTART**, **RESTORE**, **SAVE**, **SCRIPT**, **VERBOSE**, **VERSION**,
  **WAIT (Z)** — as usual, with **WAIT**'s use here being *"if you expected something
  interesting to happen at Easter, you could WAIT FOR EASTER"*, which is a fair hint about how
  this game's clock works.
* **\*MAP** — *"This will bring up the map screen in the upper window."*
* **\*REFRESH (R)** — clears the screen and redraws.
* **\*NOTIFY** — *"Normally in Arthur, the game will notify you when your score changes. You can
  turn off this notification feature by using the NOTIFY command. Typing NOTIFY a second time
  turns the feature back on."*
* **\*UNDO** — *"back up one move"*. The manual's worked example is worth repeating because it is
  the whole reason this matters in a game with a three-day clock: *"Suppose, for example, that
  you found a package but didn't know what was in it. You might type OPEN THE PACKAGE and be told
  'An evil mist escapes from the package and destroys all your possessions.' You could then type
  UNDO, and you would 'back up' one move… Note that the UNDO command works only on certain
  computers with enough memory."*
* **BRIEF is not the default here** — unlike some releases: *"Arthur will begin in VERBOSE mode
  (see VERBOSE below) and will switch to BRIEF mode only if you type BRIEF."* So the first
  description of every room is the full one, every time, until you ask otherwise.

**Abbreviations**, as the manual's list gives them: `D` down, `E` east, `G` again, `I`
inventory, `L` look, `N` north, `NE` northeast, `NW` northwest, `O` oops, `Q` quit, `R` refresh,
`S` south, `SE` southeast, `SW` southwest, `U` up, `W` west, **`X` examine**, `Z` wait.

The Table of Contents also shows what a player had beyond the pages here: *Using the New
Interface*, *Mapping*, *If You Have a Mouse*, *Hints*, *Special Commands*, *Abbreviations*,
*Sample Transcript and Map*, *About the Author*, then the usual Infocom manual sections — basic
and complex sentences, talking to characters, *Tips for Novices* (eleven pointers), *Common
Complaints*, *We're Never Satisfied*, technical problems, and a Quick Reference Guide on page 15.

## The sample transcript and map

The manual prints a worked transcript with a sketch map, *"not from Arthur, but it will give you
a good feeling for how Infocom's interactive fiction works"* — a **Clearing** with a woodsman
roasting a deer, a **Path**, a **Meadow** with a **Small box**, a **Tower Room** with a **Jewel
Girl**, a **Corridor**, and a hedged exit east that kills you. It is useful less for its puzzle
than for the shape of an Infocom answer and the replies a player learned to expect:

* `ASK WOODSMAN FOR MEAT` → *"'Well now,' says the woodsman. 'There's enuf 'ere for two, but me
  father told me never to give up sump'n for nut'n. 'Ave ye got anything to offer?'"*
* `TRADE BOX FOR MEAT` → the swap, and *"You wolf it down and your hunger disappears."*
* `THANK WOODSMAN` → *"'No need for thanks,' the woodsman says, holding up the box. 'It's a fair
  enough trade.'"*
* `OPEN IT` on the box → *"Inside is a small scrap of paper"*, and `EXAMINE PAPER` gives the
  illegible note with its two magic words: *"When thou wishest to cause something to lose
  dimension, utter the ancient word 'Twerplotz'. Contrariwise, if thou desirest to cause a thing
  to become enlarged, then shalt thou say 'Blimpsnart.'"*
* Trying to walk into the thorn hedge anyway ends in the honest way Infocom games often end:
  *"You pry yourself free and do a quick impression of a sieve leaking blood, then fall to the
  greensward, dead."*, followed by *"***** You have died. *****"* and **`[Do you wish to
  RESTART, RESTORE, UNDO, QUIT, or get a HINT?]`** — that last list is, on the evidence of this
  release, the game's answer to dying: it offers, among the usual options, to hand you the
  hints.
* And then `UNDO` → *`[Undone]`*, and `CUT HEDGE WITH SWORD` → *"For every branch you chop off,
  two grow back in its place. The woodsman chuckles knowingly."*

## The Book of Hours

A small illuminated manuscript in the box, in the style of a medieval book of hours: a title page
with a tonsured scribe at his desk beneath a sky divided between crescent moon and sun, ringed by
the names of the offices — **Vespers, Compline, Matins, Lauds, Prime, Terce, Sext, None** — and
then a page for each office, an illumination above and a four-line verse below.

**The bookplate is King Lot's**, and it is the best thing in the box for a player: *EX LIBRIS
LOTUSIAN REX*, in a frame of twisted columns with a Celtic border, opposite a verse that is the
villain's character sketch —

> No worthier warrior was before
> Lot became Lord of all the land;
> with sword so sharp and shield that shines . . .
> proud Paladin beyond reproach.
>
> He husbands heavy chests of treasure,
> the envy of every earl and king.
> His soul is stirred by precious stones and silver,
> and gleaming gold which daily he doth gather.
>
> No king so fair
> by maid begot
> was ever there
> than good King Lot.

The offices carry the ordinary devotions of a monastic day — *Matins*: *"The tocsin tolls the
time for Midnight prayers when the world's withdrawn in dreams…"*; *Lauds*: *"The Lord will
listen as our lips show forth His praise in psalm and song…"*; *Prime*: *"Whatever tempests have
tormented them outside our walls, we watch the waste with pity and compassion. All the poor we
will relieve, receiving them like rich, God-guided guests whose gift is gratitude."*; *Terce*:
*"In the scriptorium the scribes, inspired to beauty and the bounty of the Book, create with
colours and calligraphy another world to win souls and woo hearts with precious manuscripts… to
Paradise."*; *Sext*: *"At Midday, our one meal… then meditation on holy text and teaching at the
table. Since labouring is prayer, we long to linger at our task of love: to thank the Lord for
giving us the grace for gratitude."*; *None*: *"Brothers and lay labour lovingly in kitchen;
cooking, baking, making medicines. From the fields and gardens, fulsome harvest to feed ourselves
and freely share what from God's gifts we gather humbly as His servants."*

It is a book of hours with a king's ex libris pasted in it, in other words: the ordinary life of a
monastery, and, pasted over it, the man who took the sword.
